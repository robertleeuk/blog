The Geography of Bliss - Bhutan

Eric Weiner

- Shangri-La has all the components of a classic paradise. First, it's difficult to find. A paradise you can reach by simply taking a taxi is not a paradise. Moreover, paradise must be clearly distinguished from ordinary life. There must be something like the underworld between the two that only a fortunate few can cross. In other words, paradise is a club for the chosen few. Like the business class of an airplane.
- We pay homage to love and happiness. And, believe it or not, we pay homage to productivity. But we rarely say anything good about "paying attention." It seems we live too busy lives. But without attention, our lives are empty and meaningless.
- "Even if my mother is human now, I could be reborn as a dog when I die. Then my mother would be a female dog. Therefore, I must think of all living beings as my parents. I have infinite parents. I must not let my parents suffer."
- The meaning of "happiness" they think of is very different from what Americans think of—a happiness like a bubble-bursting smile symbol. For Bhutanese people, happiness means collective effort.
- Western people, especially Americans, try to eliminate the need for compromise. Cars have separate "personal temperature controls" so drivers and passengers don't have to compromise with each other over the right temperature. (Omitted) We gladly accept these technologies. Whether it's a car or a bed, why shouldn't we enjoy creating the most comfortable environment for ourselves? But I wonder if we're losing something while living so comfortably. If we no longer need to compromise over trivial things like mattresses, what about truly important issues? Compromise is a skill. And like all skills, it deteriorates if you don't use it.
- "Happiness is about relationships, and Western people think you need money to build relationships. But that's not true. What matters is trust." I heard the same thing in Switzerland. That trust is a prerequisite for happiness. Not just trust in government and institutions, but also trust in neighbors. In fact, research shows that trust is the most important factor affecting happiness—more influential than income or even health.
- He picked up each object one by one, felt genuine wonder, and examined it carefully. "But this person had no desire to buy and own things," Linda said. "He was completely satisfied just by looking at the objects in wonder, putting them back down, and leaving."
- I would live exactly the same way if I were born again. Every moment of my life, every person I've met so far, every trip I've taken, every success I've achieved, every mistake I've made, every misfortune I've experienced—they all fit me perfectly. That doesn't mean they were all good or that there was a good reason for all of them. I don't believe in vulgar fatalism. But everything I've experienced so far has fit me perfectly. So... it was okay. I know it's a rather deflating thing to call it enlightenment. "Okay" doesn't mean happiness. It can't be the foundation for a new religion or self-help movement. Oprah Winfrey wouldn't invite me at this level. But "okay" is a starting point. For just that, I am grateful.
